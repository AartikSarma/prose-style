# General prose

For replies, explanations, answers, recommendations, reports, arguments, manuscripts, and grants.

The goal is clear, vigorous writing. No single guide delivers it. This file blends five, each placed where it is strongest, in the order the decisions actually arise:

| Decide | Section | Chiefly from |
|---|---|---|
| What the prose is for | 1. Stance | Thomas & Turner, Pinker |
| Where the point goes | 2. Shape | Williams |
| How sentences order information | 3. Order | Williams |
| Which words survive | 4. Words | Strunk, Orwell |
| How it sounds | 5. Rhythm | White |
| When to break a rule | 6. The limit | Strunk; Orwell, in `SKILL.md` |

Work down the list. A sentence-level fix cannot rescue a passage that failed at stance or shape, and most unreadable prose failed early.

## 1. Stance: what the prose is for

Classic style, in Thomas and Turner's account: prose is a window onto something real. The writer has already looked and now shows the reader what they saw. The reader is an equal who would reach the same conclusion from the same view.

Three consequences.

**You have already done the work.** Do not perform the effort. The reader wants the finding, not the search.

**Beware the curse of knowledge** (Pinker). Having just read the file, you cannot feel what it is like not to have read it. This is the root cause of unreadable expert prose - not vocabulary, but the silent assumption that what you just learned was always obvious. Name the thing before you use it. Give the number, not the adjective.

**Mark the evidence.** Distinguish what you verified, what you inferred, and what you recall. Three words will do: *the file says*, *I ran it*, *from memory, so check*. Written flat, all three read as equally certain, and the reader must either trust everything or discount everything.

## 2. Shape: where the point goes

**State the point where the reader will find it: first.** A paragraph is coherent when its sentences share a small set of topics and one point governs them. A reply is coherent when its first sentence answers the question.

*Diagnostic (Williams).* Underline the point sentence. If none exists, write one. If it sits at the end of a long paragraph, the reader read the whole paragraph without knowing what it was for. Move it up.

**One paragraph, one topic.** Open with the claim or the transition; close on the strongest note.

**Prefer prose to bullets.** Bullets suit parallel items of equal weight. They destroy cause, contrast, and consequence, which is most of what an explanation carries. Three related facts belong in a sentence.

## 3. Order: how sentences carry information

**Characters as subjects, actions as verbs** (Williams). A sentence is clear when the subject names who acts and the verb names the act. Prose goes opaque when the action hides in an abstract noun and the verb slot fills with *is*, *has*, *makes*, *performs*, or *provides*.

*Diagnostic.* Underline the first seven or eight words. No concrete character and no real action means revise.

| Nominalized | Recovered |
|---|---|
| Our discussion of the problem led to a resolution. | We discussed the problem and resolved it. |
| The failure of the parser is due to an absence of validation. | The parser fails because nothing validates the input. |
| Performance of the analysis was done by the script. | The script analyzed the data. |

Signals: nouns in *-tion*, *-ment*, *-ance*, *-ity*, *-ness*; *is* linking two abstractions; the empty subjects *there is* and *it is*. Keep the nominalization when it names something already known, refers back to the previous sentence, or is the accepted term - *regression*, *randomization*.

**Use the passive when the character is unknown, irrelevant, or already known; otherwise use the active.** The passive fails when it hides who acted. It succeeds when no one needs to know. Write *we measured the flow rate*, not *measurements were obtained* - but *the samples were stored at -80 C* is correct and needs no actor.

**Old information first, new information last.** The opening words tell the reader what the sentence is about, so put there what they already know. The end carries the greatest weight, so put the news there: the point, the number, the name, the consequence.

*Diagnostic.* Read only the first four words of each sentence down a paragraph. If they form no thread, the paragraph will feel disconnected however well each sentence is polished. Then read the last four words of each. If they are not what the reader should carry away, move what is.

This is why signposting is unnecessary. "As mentioned above" patches a broken topic thread. Fix the thread and the patch is redundant. Connect with logic instead - *because*, *but*, *so*, *which means*.

**Get to the verb early; put complexity at the end.** The reader holds a sentence in memory until subject meets verb. Long subjects and interruptions force them to carry an unresolved load.

| Front-loaded | Reordered |
|---|---|
| That the parser silently drops malformed rows rather than raising is the problem. | The problem is that the parser silently drops malformed rows rather than raising. |

**Keep related words together, and express coordinate ideas in similar form** (Strunk). Position declares relationship; broken parallelism signals a difference that is not there.

## 4. Words: which ones survive

**Omit needless words** (Strunk, Rule 17). In full:

> Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts. This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that he avoid all detail and treat his subjects only in outline, but that every word tell.

The third sentence limits the second. The rule does not ask for short sentences or thin treatment; it asks that every word do work.

*Cut in this order* (Williams): words meaning nothing (*actually*, *basically*, *really*, *various*); words implied by others (*each and every*, *free gift*); phrases replaceable by one word; metadiscourse (*it should be noted that*); and anything belaboring the obvious.

| Padding | Write |
|---|---|
| the question as to whether | whether |
| the fact that he had not succeeded | his failure |
| owing to the fact that | because |
| in spite of the fact that | though |
| at this point in time | now |
| in order to | to |
| it is important to note that | (delete) |
| he is a man who | he |

Strunk calls *the fact that* "an especially debilitating expression" and asks that it be cut from every sentence in which it occurs.

**Be specific and concrete.** "It rained every day for a week" beats "A period of unfavorable weather set in." Prefer the particular to the general, the number to the adjective.

**Put statements in positive form.** Assert. Use *not* for denial, not for evasion.

**Cut qualifiers.** *Rather*, *very*, *little*, *pretty* are the leeches of prose. Delete them and the sentence firms up. Stacked hedges - *may be worth potentially considering* - are worse: pick one modal or none. But do not cut a qualification that would change the reader's decision. That word is working.

**Prefer the short familiar word,** unless the long one is genuinely more precise. *Use*, not *utilize*. *Begin*, not *commence*. But *nephrectomy*, not *kidney removal operation*, when writing for surgeons.

**Avoid dead metaphors** (Orwell). Figures worn smooth by print have stopped meaning anything: *leverage*, *surface* as a verb, *robust*, *seamless*, *deep dive*, *low-hanging fruit*. Either use a live image or say the thing plainly.

**Use the everyday word for a general reader and the technical term for a specialist.** For a specialist, the everyday equivalent is usually less precise, and precision outranks familiarity.

**Use singular *they*** for a person whose pronouns are unstated. A name is not evidence of pronouns.

Garner's *Modern English Usage* settles any disputed usage this file does not; his Language-Change Index rates how far a prescription has been overtaken.

## 5. Rhythm

White's subject, and the one Strunk never addresses. Uniform sentence length exhausts a reader even when every sentence is clean. Vary it. A short sentence after two long ones lands hard.

Read the passage aloud, or as near as you can. Prose that resists the voice will resist the eye.

Do not overwrite, and do not affect a breezy manner. Enthusiasm is not content. When a sentence resists revision, the thought behind it is usually unclear - fix the thought.

## 6. The limit

Every rule above is a means to clarity and force, never an end. Compression can overshoot: a clipped sentence that makes the reader reconstruct your meaning has failed the same test as a padded one, from the other side. Deleted hedges become false confidence. Deleted transitions become disconnected paragraphs.

Judge the result, not the compliance. The governing rule in `SKILL.md` applies here.
