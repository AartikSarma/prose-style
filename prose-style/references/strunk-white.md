# General prose: Strunk and White

For manuscripts, abstracts, grants, discussion, explanation, argument, narrative, and chat replies.

The aim is force. A reader should finish each sentence knowing more than when they started, and should never have to read one twice.

## The governing rules

**Omit needless words** (Rule 17; Rule 13 in Strunk's 1918 original). In full:

> Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts. This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that he avoid all detail and treat his subjects only in outline, but that every word tell.

The third sentence is the limit on the second, and it governs revision. The rule does not ask for short sentences or thin treatment. It asks that every word do work. A long sentence in which every word tells passes; a short one carrying a needless qualifier fails.

Two corollaries follow. Do not cut a qualification that would change the reader's decision - that word is doing work. Do not cut so far that the prose turns curt: a clipped sentence that forces the reader to reconstruct the meaning has failed the same test from the other side.

This is the rule that does the most work. Apply it last, and apply it hard.

Strunk singles out *the fact that* as "an especially debilitating expression" and asks that it be excised from every sentence in which it occurs.

Common padding, with the fix:

| Padding | Write |
|---|---|
| the question as to whether | whether |
| there is no doubt but that | doubtless |
| used for fuel purposes | used for fuel |
| he is a man who | he |
| in a hasty manner | hastily |
| this is a subject that | this subject |
| the fact that he had not succeeded | his failure |
| owing to the fact that | since, because |
| in spite of the fact that | though, although |
| call your attention to the fact that | remind you |
| the reason why is that | because |
| at this point in time | now |
| in order to | to |
| it is important to note that | (delete) |

**Use the active voice.** "The committee approved the plan" beats "The plan was approved by the committee." The passive earns its place only when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately withheld, or when the sentence's true subject is the thing acted upon.

**Put statements in positive form.** Assert. "He usually came late" beats "He was not very often on time." Use *not* as a means of denial, not of evasion.

**Use definite, specific, concrete language.** Prefer the particular to the general, the concrete to the abstract. "A period of unfavorable weather set in" is dead; "It rained every day for a week" is alive. Distrust nouns built from verbs - *utilization*, *implementation*, *determination*. Recover the verb inside.

**Put the emphatic word at the end.** The end of the sentence is the position of greatest force; the beginning is second. Bury qualifications in the middle. This single move does more for readability than any other revision.

## Structure

**Make the paragraph the unit of composition.** One topic per paragraph. Begin with a sentence that announces the topic or helps the transition, and end on the strongest note.

**Keep related words together.** The position of words in a sentence declares their relationship. A modifier belongs beside what it modifies.

**Express coordinate ideas in similar form.** Parallel construction signals parallel meaning; broken parallelism signals a difference that is not there.

**Use one tense throughout a summary.** Shifting tense makes the reader re-establish footing.

**Place the emphatic element of the paragraph last**, as with the sentence.

## Voice

**Write naturally, and do not overwrite.** Rich, ornate prose is exhausting. So is deliberate flatness.

**Avoid qualifiers.** *Rather*, *very*, *little*, *pretty* are the leeches of prose. Delete them and the sentence firms up.

**Do not explain too much.** Adverbs after "he said" are usually a confession that the dialogue is weak. The same holds for adverbs propping up a weak verb.

**Do not affect a breezy manner.** Enthusiasm is not a substitute for content.

**Avoid fancy words.** Prefer the short word to the long, the Anglo-Saxon to the Latinate, the familiar to the obscure - unless the long word is genuinely more precise. *Use*, not *utilize*. *Begin*, not *commence*. But *nephrectomy*, not *kidney removal operation*, when writing for surgeons.

**Be clear.** When a sentence resists revision, the thought behind it is usually unclear. Fix the thought.

**Do not inject opinion** where the reader wants fact.

**Prefer the standard to the offbeat.** Novel usage draws attention to the writer and away from the subject.

## Scientific prose

The rules above hold, with three additions that matter for manuscripts and grants:

- **Claim what your data support, and no more.** Match hedging to evidence. Uniform hedging tells the reader nothing.
- **Name the actor in methods and results.** "We measured" is honest and shorter than "measurements were obtained."
- **Lead each paragraph of a discussion with its claim,** not with a review of prior work. The reader should learn your point before your justification.
